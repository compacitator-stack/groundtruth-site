The Filter We Skipped — Qullamaggie’s Strong-Market, Hot-Sector, Market-Wide-Leader Filter, Applied by a Machine

Episode A03b · report format v2 · lab run 2026-09-14, pre-registration locked 2026-09-14 before the run
VERDICT — APPLIED MECHANICALLY, THE FILTER MADE EVERY MEASURED NUMBER WORSE — IN SAMPLE AND OUT. THE MECHANICS UNDER IT ARE REAL.

A viewer on our first Qullamaggie episode said the breakout only works in strong markets and hot sectors, and that we had skipped that filter. He was half right about us: the strong-market gate was in the first test; the market-wide leader rank and the hot-sector rung were not, and our own lab had written both down as gaps. So we froze the first episode’s mechanics, pre-registered five gates, and built the filter as he states it on his nightly scans. Each rung made the same pile of trades worse. The plain entry: 783 trades, 32.2% win, +0.25 R average, profit factor 0.87. Add the strong-market gate: 717, 30.0%, +0.28 R, 0.84. Add the market-wide leader rank: 222, 26.6%, -0.19 R, 0.70. Add the hot sector: 78, 24.4%, -0.33 R, 0.55. 783 in, 78 out, and the profit factor fell at every rung. Both halves of the window lose, the three best names were the only thing holding it up, no dial setting reached break-even, and the 2010–2015 holdout — declared before the run, run once — agrees. The verdict is a pair: the mechanics are real and his signature is in every cell; the filter, as written and applied without judgment, is not an edge.

What we granted, and what we actually tested

Granted, not measured: by his own published record and by our first episode’s verdict, that Kristjan Qullamaggie trades this profitably, and that the published breakout mechanics reproduce the profile he describes — a low win rate and a fat tail, the median trade losing about what it risked. None of that is on trial here. His read of which theme is driving a group, his entries during the trading day and his sizing by conviction are not written down as rules, so they are not in the code, and a filter without them is his filter, not his edge.

Tested: one thing: whether the filter the viewer named — as Qullamaggie himself states it on his nightly scans (the stock in the top 7% of the whole market by return over one, three or six months; in an industry group whose three-month return is in the top quarter of groups; in an uptrending market) — recovers an edge for the published entry when applied mechanically. Five arms on one footing: the control, then each rung added, then the sector rung on its own to isolate which half carries anything. 38 trade ledgers, 7,734 trades, all of them below or downloadable, none dropped.

What this report does NOT claim, pre-committed before the data: this measures the rule, not the trader. It does not measure his discretionary judgment and cannot. It does not establish why the filter hurts: our read (a top-7% leader has already run, so the breakout-day stop sits under a more extended price and the fat tail shrinks) is labelled as a read below, never as a measurement. It does not test a sector classification better than the two-digit SIC code, which is coarse — the reopen criteria name that first. And “strong market” was already in the first test; this page re-runs it on the same footing rather than pretending the viewer’s first half was new.

§1 · The locked pre-registration

Committed d755517 on 2026-09-14, before the probe was run — the wager you cannot move after seeing the cards.

The falsifiable question

Does the strong-market + market-wide-leader + hot-sector filter, applied mechanically to the published breakout entry on a point-in-time, survivorship-free universe, recover a positive edge — and does that edge survive both halves of the window, the removal of its three best names, every neighbouring dial setting, and a holdout the filter never saw?

  • A yes looks like: the full filter clears PF 1.3 with an average trade of +0.30 R or better on at least 150 trades, holds in both halves and without its top three names, keeps PF above 1.0 at every dial setting, and reads PF 1.1 or better on the 2010–2015 holdout.
  • A no looks like: the filter reads at or below the plain entry on the pre-registered rulers, or clears the first gate in sample and dies out of sample — the pattern this channel has watched repeatedly.
  • Ambiguous residue: an arm where the two rulers disagree — a positive average trade beside a profit factor under one. That happened on the control and on the sector-only arm, and it is reported as two rulers printed side by side, never as one of them read alone.

Our stated prior: a coin flip on the first gate, fifteen in a hundred on all four

Written beside the pass mark before the run: P(G1) about 0.5 — a leader filter should raise the average trade in sample — and P(G1 through G4) about 0.15, because this channel has watched in-sample lifts die out of sample before and the second half of the window holds a bear market. The likeliest honest story we wrote down was that the filter beats the control in sample, the lift is carried by three names or by the 2020–21 stretch, and the holdout kills it. The result embarrassed the prior in the other direction: the full filter did not beat the control on any registered measure even on the data it was built on.

§3 · Was it the path? The stress tests

Is it one era? No. Cut the 7.5-year window in two at 2022-09-30 and the full filter reads PF 0.46 in the first half (39 trades) and 0.68 in the second (39). Both halves losing, so it is not a story about one stretch of one market.

Is it one name? No. In the first episode a single stock was 80% of a whole window’s profit, so this is a house rule now: pull the three largest net contributors (RVMD, ASAN, TEAM, re-ranked from the ledger at build) and the remaining 74 trades read PF 0.29. There was no good filter hiding under a few unlucky trades; the three best names were the only thing holding a bad one up.

Is it the dial? No. The leader cut at 2, 5, 7 and 10 percent; the group cut at the top third, quarter and tenth: 6 of 6 settings of the full filter read under break-even (best 0.97, on 8 and 39 trades; worst 0.50), and the setting that looked best in the first half (full filter, leader 2%) is not the one that looked best in the second (full filter, leader 5%) — the fingerprint of noise, not of a filter.

And the three cells in the wider grid that did touch 1.0 — each paired with its holdout read, never shown alone. leader 5%, no sector rung: PF 1.42 on 106 trades in sample → 0.52 on 120 on the holdout; full filter, sector by the 11 SPDR funds: PF 1.01 on 115 trades in sample → 0.74 on 87 on the holdout; sector only, sector by the 11 SPDR funds: PF 1.01 on 558 trades in sample → 0.86 on 279 on the holdout. §5 · The best objection, pre-registered and answered

“You tested a caricature. His hot sector is a judgment, not a SIC code.” Correct, and it is granted above rather than argued with. We tested the rule as he states it on his scans, applied by a machine, because that is the only version a test can reach; a ledger can tell you what the rule does and cannot tell you what the trader does. The two-digit SIC code is coarse — on a typical day only about 13 groups had the 10 members needed to rank at all (about 6 on the holdout), and 6,471 of 19,317 qualifying breakouts sat in a group too small to count, so the sector rung never saw them. A better point-in-time sector map is the first thing that reopens this page. The SPDR-fund variant is the one cut at a coarser map we could run, and it is in the grid above with its holdout read.

“Seven percent is arbitrary.” It is his number, from his own scans, and it was registered before the run. The objection is answered by the dial grid: 2, 5, 7 and 10 percent all read under 1.0 for the full filter, and the one setting that looked good without the sector rung (leader 5%, no sector rung) read 0.52 on the holdout.

“Seven years is one market.” Agreed, which is why the 2010–2015 holdout was declared before the first cell ran and run once: 1,510 sessions on a smaller market (219 members on a typical day). The full filter read PF 0.54 there against the plain entry’s 0.73, and the sector-only arm 0.41. The same direction, on data the filter had never seen.

§6 · Their own terms

Six things on this page run against us. We would rather print them than have them found.

1. The holdout was run once, but its result was not committed-before-run in the way the pre-registration promised. The pre-registration said the holdout would run after every in-sample number was written to the results file and committed. The run log shows one holdout phase and nothing touched after it, and the results write-up was on disk before that phase began (the runner refuses to start the holdout otherwise); what did not happen is the commit of that write-up before the holdout ran. The ONCE half held; the COMMIT-before-holdout half did not, and this page says so rather than implying it.

2. The two rulers disagree on two arms. The control’s average trade is +0.25 R beside a profit factor of 0.87; the sector-only arm’s is +0.38 R beside 0.69. Sizing is not uniform across trades, and we did not chase the split. Nothing on this page reads either arm on one ruler.

3. The sector map is coarse and a third of the breakouts never reached the sector rung. 6,471 of 19,317 qualifying signals sat in groups under the 10-member floor; SIC coverage was 95.1% of member-days. A limit of the mechanisation, stated.

4. Three grid cells touched 1.0 in sample. They are printed with their holdout reads rather than omitted, and none of the three survived. The wider the grid, the more likely a green cell; that is the reason the holdout was sealed first.

5. The regime gate is the ported code, not the one-line summary the pre-registration wrote for it. The summary said SPY and QQQ above their 20- and 50-day averages; the function that ran gates on SPY above its 50- and 200-day averages with a recovery override and cuts slots when QQQ is below its 50-day. The rule to re-use the exact code outranked the summary; the deviation is logged in the pre-registration’s appendix.

6. “Forty-four files” counts the whole results folder. The lab’s results folder holds 44 files; 38 of them are trade ledgers, one row per trade (7,734 rows in all), and the other six are the machine-readable results, the run log and three console captures. §9 · What survives

The breakout itself, exactly as the first episode left it: strength, a tight base, a stop you can name, a few large winners paying for the rest. The first episode’s one law survives too — every safety rule you bolt on has to be checked against the rare winner it might delete. And the viewer’s instinct survives, because it was right about something real: which stocks you run this on matters more than any rule in the file. What did not survive is the idea that a rule can do that choosing for you.

The portable part is one question, and it costs two columns of a spreadsheet: before you add a filter to anything — a strategy, a screen, a bot — ask what it does to the average trade on the trades it kept, and whether the profit factor agrees. Fewer trades is not the same as better trades, and it takes both rulers to tell those apart. When they split, you have not earned the filter yet. Then run the same two columns on a window the filter never saw.
