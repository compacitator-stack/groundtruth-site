The Method — the Five Gates

1Get the claim in writing. Exact promise, exact rules. If the rules aren't public, that's the first finding.
2Real data, including the graveyard. Survivorship-free, point-in-time — the accounts that blew up don't post returns.
3Real costs. Commissions, slippage, spreads, the fills you actually get.
4Write pass/fail down before the results. Pre-registration — or the test grades its own homework.
5Publish what would change your mind. A verdict without a reopen condition is an opinion.

Is JEPI Worth It? We Measured It Against the Thing It’s Made Of

Episode D02Income ETFs VERDICT: not a kill — a dispersion. Measured as a simple yearly rate, the covered-call wrapper always cost something over each fund’s own life against the thing it owns: 0 of 21 measured pairs kept pace, from about two points a year at the cheap end to a fund where a dollar became $1.28 against $2.07 in its own stock — and the headline yield did not tell you which one you had bought

Twenty-four income ETFs, 21 of them priced against their own underlying over their whole life, on survivorship-free month-end records through 2026-06-30, every cell re-derived (96 of 96 master cells, 21 of 21 cap-cost cells, 0 diffs). JEPI, the $44.3B fund that defines the category, paid 8.8% a year and gave up 7.9 points a year to SPY — within a point of each other, two measurements and no mechanism — so a dollar became $1.83 against $2.67 over the same 6.1 years. The cheapest call-writers cost 2.2–2.8 points; GPIX gave up 3.3 and grew NAV 13.8% a year; QYLD over 12.5 years became $1.76 against $9.26. The wild end ships as dollars, not rates, because our own “CAGR” column is an annualized arithmetic mean — and on the compounding ruler MSTY beat its own stock, which we found only after correcting an extra index month on every pair. 17 of 24 funds have never seen a bear. Three scars on our own instrument, all 24 rows, no specimen’s figures, and the three things that would change our mind.

The Truth About Dividend Investing — And the Metric That Decides Nothing

Episode D17Dividend investing VERDICT: the metric is empty and the strategy is fine — yield on cost is today’s yield times the price ratio since purchase, an identity we rendered on 184 of 184 fund-months at floating-point zero; the two dividend funds we measured compounded for real and still finished behind the index (a dollar in SCHD became $5.95 against $7.68 in SPY over 14.7 years); he is right to hold, for the tax reason he gave second

A Canadian income-ETF educator holds his fund because his yield on cost is 22.6% against a 15.1% market yield. We rendered yield on cost month by month on two US funds we measured from a survivorship-free store: on SCHD the two lines separate by exactly the ×3.34 the price moved, on ULTY by the ×0.35 it collapsed, and on one day SCHD wears 166 different yields on cost, 2.45% to 8.42%, against one market yield of 2.49%. His own two figures, divided, hand back the ~50% price gain he states separately — his numbers, our arithmetic. Then the question the metric skips: a dollar in SCHD became $5.95 over its 14.7-year life against $7.68 in SPY, and $3.76 on price alone — real compounding, behind the index. Nothing about his Canadian fund is measured by us; the on-jurisdiction evidence is Ben Felix’s, credited by name. The git-witnessed pre-registration, all 184 rows, and the three things that would change our mind.

Does Backtesting Really Work? Here Is the One Thing to Check.

Episode B04Our own process VERDICT: 13 of 13 of our registered labs wrote the bar down — reading it back is the half that fails: at least 9 registered items across 6 labs were never scored, and 8 of the 9 post-publication corrections and unregistered deviations we found ran toward flattering us

We read our own pre-registrations back against our own results: 14 lab directories, 13 registrations, about 143 registered items, 27 of them hand-scored with a line reference on both sides. Every registered lab carries a priors block, and 20 of 27 hand-scored priors hit — inside the 65–85% band we registered before counting. That is the easy half. At least 9 registered items across 6 labs were never scored by the results that were supposed to score them; one lab registered 25 items and published no results at all; another published results with no registration. Our own direction prediction for this very ledger was written too loosely to say which rows it meant, so it scored 62.5% one way and 35.3% the other, and we publish both. There is no defendant here but us. The dated pre-registration (not hash-locked, and we say why), all 27 rows, and the six envelopes with their commit timestamps read from git.

The Filter We Skipped — Qullamaggie’s Strong-Market, Hot-Sector, Market-Wide-Leader Filter, Applied by a Machine

Episode A03bBreakouts VERDICT: applied mechanically, the filter made every measured number worse — in sample and out; the mechanics under it are real

A viewer on our first Qullamaggie episode said the breakout only works in strong markets and hot sectors, and that we had skipped that filter. He was half right about us. So we froze the first episode’s mechanics, pre-registered five gates, built the filter as he states it on his own nightly scans, and ran it on every US stock that met his floor from 2019, delisted names included: 783 breakouts in, 78 out, and the profit factor fell at every rung (0.87 → 0.84 → 0.70 → 0.55). Both halves lose, the three best names were the only thing holding it up, no dial setting reached break-even, and the 2010–2015 holdout, run once, agrees. The verdict is a pair: the mechanics are real; the rule, without his judgment, is not an edge. Every trade of every cell is in the ledger.

Steven Dux Said His Own Edge Was Fading. We Measured It.

Episode A10Micro-cap shorting VERDICT: it’s complicated — the rule by company size is false, the rule by year was real in 2021, and he told his audience it was fading before we did

Two pre-registrations, run against the days his own rule names. The version that sorts by company size did not survive. The version that sorts by year did: in 2021 the stocks that crossed his line closed lower 20.7 percentage points more often than the same stocks on the days they did not. By 2023, at his own number, that gap is 5.3. He disclosed the decay himself, and he was right. What we still cannot measure is whether any of it was ever borrowable — that is the open bounty.
