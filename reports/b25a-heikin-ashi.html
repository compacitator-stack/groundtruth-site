VERDICT — Our pre-registration cannot call the question it was written for

We wrote two lines before the run. The kill line needed two things: with no costs, the two charts within 0.15 Sharpe of each other; and after 10 basis points a round trip, Heikin-Ashi earning less a year than holding every stock on the list. Both came true: a Sharpe gap of -0.012, and -0.62% a year against 9.69%. The rescue line needed Heikin-Ashi to beat plain candles by 0.15 Sharpe or more after costs, and to land above the 95th percentile of the random traders. Both came true as well: an after-cost gap of +0.162, and 0.0740 against the registered crowd’s 0.0712. Our written rule says a mixed result like that is reported as inconclusive, and on 2026-09-24 the channel ruled that the rule’s literal text governs. The lab’s first write-up, dated 2026-07-14, called this a kill; that call is withdrawn (§1). What we measured does not change with the call. Before costs the picture added nothing. The money on the smooth chart comes from a price no order fills at. After costs the smooth rule lost less only because it traded less, and it still lost money, one dollar to 87 cents, while holding the same stocks earned 9.69% a year.

What we granted, and what we actually tested

Granted, not measured: The video’s account of how these candles are drawn: each body is an average of the bar’s four prices, each new body starts halfway up the one before it, and the wicks still show the high and the low. Its advice to learn plain candlesticks first, and that trading is not a get-rich-quick thing. And the possibility that a calmer chart helps a person sit through a trade: that is about nerves, which our test cannot reach, so we grant it rather than claim to have tested it.

Tested: One rule, run twice on the same stocks and the same days: hold a stock while its candle is green, sell it when the candle turns red, and split the money equally across every stock that is green. The first run read the colour from plain candles (green when the day closed above its open); the second from Heikin-Ashi candles (green when the average sits above where its body started). Daily candles, long only, 2,606 US common stocks from 2004-01-02 to 2026-06-22, with no costs and with 10 basis points a round trip. A third run used the same Heikin-Ashi rule but was paid at the Heikin-Ashi price itself, the level the chart draws, to measure how far that price sits from one that actually traded.

What this report does NOT claim: that the setup shown in the video (a particular Heikin-Ashi candle at a zone drawn by hand, aiming to make twice what it risks) works or fails, because we did not test it; anything about five-minute charts, which the video’s trades are shown on; anything about the short side; anything about other ways of redrawing candles, because we tested Heikin-Ashi only; anything about the inner circle membership the video pitches or the creator’s own trading; that a calmer chart cannot help a person’s discipline; and anything about returns after dividends or inflation, because every return here counts price alone, in nominal US dollars.

The falsifiable question

Does the Heikin-Ashi picture, by itself, give a trading rule an edge that the same rule on plain candles does not have?

  • A yes looks like: the rescue line: after costs, Heikin-Ashi beats plain candles by 0.15 Sharpe or more, and beats the 95th percentile of the random traders.
  • A no looks like: the kill line: with no costs the two charts land within 0.15 Sharpe of each other, and after costs Heikin-Ashi earns less a year than holding every stock on the list.
  • Ambiguous residue: both lines, or neither. The pre-registration says: “Mixed → report honestly as inconclusive; do not narrate a verdict.” That is the branch this test landed in.

Our stated prior: the picture adds nothing of its own

The pre-registration filed the test under the thesis that drawing candles differently is not, by itself, a sufficient edge: a transform of the same four prices repaints the picture, not the profit. The cleaning rule we added after the first run moved the no-cost comparison toward that prior, which is why §3 discloses it before anything else.

§3 · Was it the path? The stress tests

Three things could make this result an artifact of how we ran it. We checked what we could and name what we could not.

1. The cleaning rule we added after the first run

Our first run was polluted by penny stocks and dying stocks that swung by half their price or more in a single day. They broke the equal-weight benchmark, and they hit the plain-candle run much harder than the smooth one, so in that run Heikin-Ashi scored higher before costs by more than 0.15. Under the lines we wrote first, that run was inconclusive. We do not print its figures: its benchmark was broken, and a figure from a broken run is excluded, not hedged.

After seeing it, we added two cleaning rules: hold a stock only if the signal day closed at $5 or more, and count no day’s move for more than 50% either way. Before the fix the two charts scored too far apart to count as the same; after it they landed inside the 0.15 line. That moved the result toward what we expected, which is why it is disclosed here first. The rules were applied to every run and to the benchmark, and the cleaned benchmark (9.69% a year) now lands near SPY (8.85%), which the cleaning never touched. We ran one cleaning version only. A check with other price floors and other caps is owed (§8).

2. The universe: a point-in-time version, added after the run

The list pools every year’s 800 most liquid stocks into one, with delisted stocks present on the days they traded. That makes it survivorship-free, but not point-in-time: a stock can be held on days before it was first selected, 1,800,499 of 8,814,019 tradeable stock-days. On 2026-09-24 we added a sensitivity, unregistered and never a verdict layer, that holds each stock only from its first yearly selection; a stock that later drops out of the top 800 stays in, so it corrects entry only. The pattern is the same: the no-cost gap is +0.0076 (inside 0.15), the after-cost gap +0.1780, and the rule loses 3.03% a year against 6.88% for holding. Exposure keeps the full 2,606-stock denominator, so it falls when stocks enter later.

3. Dividends and the cost base

Every return here, the rule’s and the benchmarks’, counts price alone, without dividends; that is why SPY reads only 8.85% a year. Which way the missing dividends cut between the rule and the benchmarks was never measured, and we make no claim about it. The rule’s turnover statistic (144.6 a year) differs from its book turnover, total changes over total stocks held times 252 (127.1). Costs are charged on the first. Which is the better cost base was not evaluated.

§4 · The controls

The core control is the plain-candle twin: the same rule, stocks, days, fill and costs, with only the candle that decides green or red changed. The tag run measures the illusion. The benchmarks are equal-weight buy-and-hold of every stock on the list, and SPY. On 2026-09-24 the lab’s own functions reproduced 33 of 33 published cells to within one millionth before any new crowd was drawn, and for this report they rebuilt the published cells again (§10).

The random traders

The pre-registration asked for 200 random traders, each holding each stock for the same share of time as the rule, but on random days, and, by its general friction line (its line on the crowd names no cost), paying the same cost per trade as the rule. The crowd the published run built had 120 traders and shuffled over every day in the panel, including days a stock was not listed, so its traders held stocks on fewer days than the rule (exposure 0.249 against 0.314, measured on the 2026-09-24 replay of its draws). On 2026-09-24 we rebuilt it as registered: 200 traders, each stock held for the rule’s share of its own listed days. That rebuilt crowd is the registered one, and it is the one the episode speaks.

Picking days at random means switching far more often. On our turnover statistic, the registered crowd traded about 1.7 times as often as the rule (247.0 against 144.6 a year). On the plain count of position changes, measured on its first draw only, it made 1.96 times as many (4,565,204 against 2,329,467). The first figure is the per-day turnover statistic the costs are charged on; the second is a trade count. They measure different things.

After the run, and unregistered, we added a second crowd that trades about as often as the rule: each stock’s own sequence of holds, rotated to a random start, which keeps the length of every holding run but the one that wraps. It matches the rule’s trade count (2,331,070 against 2,329,467 position changes on its first draw), not the turnover statistic (127.3 against 144.6 a year; its construction check failed, a 12% difference against a 5% limit). On that statistic it pays the smaller bill: 6.36 points a year against the rule’s 7.23. It is never a verdict layer.

Each trader shuffles the days for each stock separately, which breaks up how long each holding lasts and loses the way stocks tend to rise together; a shuffle that keeps the stocks moving together was not run, so we cannot say which way that cuts. 3.86% of the registered crowd’s held stock-days had a signal day under the $5 floor the rule obeys; the pre-registration is silent on whether the crowd should obey it.

§5 · The strongest objections, and what we did not measure

Daily candles, not five-minute ones. The trades in the video are shown on five-minute charts; this test used daily candles. We have not measured the gap between the tag and the till on five-minute charts, and a pre-registered five-minute test is the first thing that could settle the question (§8).

Our fill is kind to the rule. We filled at the close that made the signal: a price that really traded, but one no order could act on in time, because a candle is only known to be green once the close has printed. Our cost charge also runs low on days of heavy selling, because the changed fraction of the book is capped at 100%. Both favour the rule, and it still lost money.

The cleaning rule moved the result toward our prior. Disclosed in §3 with the one cleaning version we ran; the check with other floors and caps is owed.

The entry reading could be wrong. §6 gives our reading of the video’s entry sentence as ours, and says what has not been checked by sight.

§6 · Their own terms

The video is “Heikin Ashi” Are The Easiest Way To Become Profitable FAST (Jooviers Gems, 2026-07-08). Its words below are quoted exactly, checked word for word against a transcript of its 501 captions that we stored, with one exception: in the 15:40 line the transcript has the word the twice in a row, and we print it once. Times are caption starts. Automatic captions spell Heikin-Ashi seven different ways, and we write it one way.

  • 0:00 “Switching to Heikin-Ashi candlesticks is by far the easiest way to become profitable fast and they work so well it almost feels like cheating.”
  • 0:18 “you're going to know exactly what Heikin-Ashi candlesticks are, as well as how they are better than regular candlesticks.”
  • 4:03 “You can hit this dropdown on trading view or any platform you use and just press Heikin-Ashi candlesticks.”
  • 4:45 “they're very widely used in Japan.”
  • 6:25 “The top of a bullish candlestick's body is showing us the average of where price has been throughout this entire candlestick.”
  • 8:33 “it stops you from getting scared of getting out of a trade too early when you see one candlestick go against you.”
  • 10:57 “you should learn regular candlesticks first.”
  • 15:40 “Remember also that the body does not show where price closed at.”
  • 15:52 “I like to put my entry as if it closed at the top of the candlestick.”

Our test is aimed at the claim about the chart itself: that the smoothing makes the rule pay. The video’s account of how the candles are drawn is right, and we used the same average. Its point about nerves is one our test cannot reach.

The entry sentence. The video says the body does not show where price closed; twelve seconds later it places the entry as if the candle closed at its top. Our reading, and it is ours, not the creator’s: on a Heikin-Ashi chart the top of a green body is an average of the bar’s four prices, so an entry placed there is placed at the tag, not the till. The video uses top or bottom of a candlestick for a body edge in 8 of 8 earlier uses, and says wick when it means the high or the low.

Not confirmed by sight. The frames at 15:38–16:00, where the entry is drawn, have not been checked: no copy of the video is on our disk and we did not download one. If the marker sits at the top of the wick, the entry is a price that traded and our reading is wrong. The transcript also never says the candle’s colour; on a red Heikin-Ashi candle the top of the body is its open, which is an average too.

§8 · What would change our mind

  1. A test that clears the tightened bar. A pre-registered test, on five-minute or daily candles, that uses the same trading costs in every comparison, in which a Heikin-Ashi rule beats its plain-candle twin by 0.15 Sharpe or more after costs AND beats the 95th percentile of random traders who trade as often as it does, with every fill at a price an order placed in time could have got. Meet that, and we will say on this channel that the smooth chart adds an edge of its own. Where our daily test stands against it: it clears 0.15 after costs (+0.162); it is above 0 of 200 draws of the crowd that trades about as often (M2, added after the run, matched on trade count); and its close fill does not meet the fill condition.
  2. The structural condition. Heikin-Ashi is made from the same four prices, so any such win cannot be new information. It would mean this particular smoothing works as a filter that skips some trades, and it should be tested against a plain-candle rule that holds each trade just as long.
  3. A cleaning sweep. If the no-cost tie flips under other price floors ($1, $5, $10) or other caps on a day’s move (25%, 50%, none), we will say so here first.
  4. The entry frame. If the frames at 15:38–16:00 show the entry marker at the top of the wick, the reading in §6 is withdrawn.
  5. The video’s own setup. A particular Heikin-Ashi candle at a zone drawn by hand, aiming for twice what it risks, is untested. Its entries are also read off the smooth chart, but that is an argument, not a measurement, and we will say so until we run it.

§9 · What survives

The chart survives, as a way of seeing. The video’s account of how the candles are drawn survives, and so does its advice to learn plain candles first.

As for the money, we can only say what we measured. Before costs, the two charts tied; after costs, the smooth chart lost less only because it traded less. A calmer chart can improve your discipline, though our test could not measure that. By our reasoning, it cannot put an edge into prices that did not already have one: every Heikin-Ashi candle is built from the four prices the plain candle already shows.

The question to carry out of here: before you trust any chart that makes trading look easy, find the exact moment you would have known to act, and ask what price was on offer at that moment. Call it the till test. It works on a smooth candle, a backtest screenshot, or a chart in anybody’s video, including ours.
