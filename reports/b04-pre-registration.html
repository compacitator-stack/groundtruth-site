Does Backtesting Really Work? Here Is the One Thing to Check.

Episode B04 · report format v2 · lab run 2026-08-15 · a snapshot of validation/ at repo HEAD 575c72b, pre-registration dated 2026-08-15 17:52 PDT — not hash-locked
VERDICT — 13 OF 13 LABS WROTE THE BAR DOWN. READING IT BACK IS THE HALF THAT FAILS

Every lab in this repo that carries a pre-registration carries an identifiable priors or goalposts block — 13 of 13. Where we scored those priors by hand we hit 20 of 27, 74.1%, inside the 65–85% band we registered before counting. That is the easy half. The other half: 12 of 13 registered labs have a results file at all; at least 9 registered items across 6 labs are never scored by the results that were supposed to score them; one lab registered 25 items and published nothing to score them against; another published results with no pre-registration at all. And 27 of about 143 registered items in this corpus carry a hand verdict — an unscored row is not a passing row. Writing the bar down is the easy half. Reading it back afterwards is the half that fails, and our own corpus is the proof.

What we granted, and what we actually tested

Granted, not measured: that the backtest a viewer is being shown is almost certainly true — real code, real data, it reproduces, and nobody faked it. The failure named here has nothing to do with lying, and we are not testing whether anybody lied. We also grant that a criterion written after the fact can still be perfectly honest. It simply cannot be evidence, because it cannot lose.

Tested: only our own corpus. 14 lab directories under validation/ as of 2026-08-15, 13 of them carrying a pre-registration, about 143 registered items enumerated from the pre-registration side rather than from whatever the results happened to discuss, and 27 rows hand-scored with a line reference on both sides. Zero new computation and no market data: this is a file-reading census of our own paperwork.

What this report does NOT claim, pre-committed before the data: no corpus-wide count of priors we have published as misses; no claim that our hit rate is good or bad against any outside benchmark; and no claim that pre-registration improves decision accuracy — only that it makes a result capable of losing. It also does not re-run any lab or re-verify any lab's underlying figures. Every outcome here is what that lab's results file claims, read against what its pre-registration registered. A lab whose results are themselves wrong is scored as its results say.

§1 · The pre-registration — and what it cannot prove

This pre-registration is dated, not hash-locked, and we are saying so. It was written at 2026-08-15 17:52 PDT, against repo HEAD 0a05136, before any priors block or scorecard in the corpus had been opened for counting. But no commit lock exists: the authoring session had no commit authority, so the pre-registration and the results landed together, and version control cannot witness the ordering. That is an attestation, not cryptographic evidence — and it is the same limitation this report flags on one of the labs it scores. Manufacturing a hash to close the gap would be the exact defect this episode exists to name.

The falsifiable question

Across every lab in this repo: how many priors and goalposts have we registered, how did they score — and when we deviated, did the deviation run in the direction that flattered us?

  • A yes looks like: at least 60% of direction-assignable non-HIT rows understate our own case, and the overall hit rate lands inside the registered 65–85% band.
  • A no looks like: flattering deviations outnumber understating ones, or the hit rate falls outside that band. We wrote down what a death would mean before the count: a hit rate above the band would say our priors are written loose enough that they cannot lose, and a flattering direction would say the discipline is being practised as decoration in our own lab — which is the exact thing this episode condemns.
  • Ambiguous residue: the registration never said which set of rows it would be scored against. That is where the direction prediction actually landed, and it is a defect in our registration rather than a property of the corpus.

Our stated prior: our deviations would cluster toward understating our own findings

It split, and the split indicts the registration rather than rescuing it. On the 27 hand-scored rows the direction bar clears at 62.5% (5 understated against 3 flattered) — over a 60% bar by two and a half points, close enough that one row moving flips it. Add the labs' own post-publication corrections and unregistered deviations and it collapses to 35.3% (6 of 17).

We did not register which set of rows this would be scored against, so we are not permitted to pick the tier that scores better. Both are published in §5. That is an unbounded prior — the precise defect one of the labs in this very table had already written down as a lesson — and we committed it in a prior about our failure to bound priors.

One more thing we owe you about this prediction: we do not claim this was a blind prediction. It generalises a finding one of these labs had already published about itself, and the pre-registration says so, in its own contamination section, written before the count. It is still a real test — that lab's finding was about that lab, and there was no reason it had to hold across the corpus — but it was not blind and may not be described as though it were.

§4 · The controls

The enumerator has a negative control, because a guard only ever seen green is untested. Its self-test removes a registered label from a results file and asserts the diff goes red, then asserts the untouched corpus stays green for that same label. It passes both. So the mechanical check works — it is simply asking a smaller question than the finding needed, which is the paragraph above.

This report has one too. Running its generator with --selftest perturbs a single scored row and asserts the integrity gate refuses to write the page. That gate is what stands between the headline above and the table below: it recomputes the outcome tally, the direction tally, the hit rate and all four verdicts from the 27 rows, checks them against the counts the lab file already published, and refuses to emit a byte if they disagree. On a report about results that cannot lose, a page that could not reproduce its own headline from its own ledger would be self-refuting.

Independent corroboration, read back from the labs' own published scorecards: 4 hits, 1 miss; 6 hits, 1 near-hit, 1 UNSCORABLE, 0 clear misses; Prediction scorecard: 6 hit / 1 partial. Those lines corroborate the hand scoring; the 20-of-27 figure itself is assembled from the §7 rows and not from these scorecards (one of them belongs to a lab with no row in the 27), and each was opened and re-read against its own file rather than carried forward from memory.

§8 · What would change our mind

  1. Somebody who publishes a timestamped bar and then publishes a loss against it. We feature them by name, on camera, as the counterexample this report could not find. It takes all four: a verifiable pre-result timestamp; a published loss against that criterion; a criterion bounded on both sides; and a real funnel, so the bar had something to cost.
  2. Evidence that pre-registration costs accuracy, not just comfort — a demonstration that bars fixed in advance produce systematically worse decisions on the same data than post-hoc analysis does. That is a real argument against the discipline and we would run it.
  3. This ledger re-run against the blind arm the concurrent session registered. That registration covers the labs this pass had already observed and registers predictions over the remainder. A blind re-score that contradicts the direction finding in §5 would change the closing claim of this report.
  4. A registered criterion of ours that could not have failed. We had one candidate and it stopped qualifying: its own results file retracted the argument that made it unfailable, and the test now passes 9 of 1,008. Finding another one in our corpus remains an open bounty against us, and we will publish it.

§9 · What survives

The portable version is three questions, and each takes about five seconds on any backtest anybody shows you.

Where is the bar, and when was it written? Not whether the result is impressive — whether a criterion existed before the result did. If the success criterion cannot be dated earlier than the number it blesses, the number is a description, not a test.

Is the bar bounded on both sides? A criterion with an open end cannot lose in the direction the result actually lands. This is the one we failed on ourselves: our own direction prediction never said which rows it would be scored against, so it scored 62.5% one way and 35.3% the other, and we had no principled way to choose.

Which way do the corrections run? Everybody publishes errata. The question is whether they are random-signed. Ours are not: 8 of the 9 post-publication corrections and unregistered deviations we found moved toward the version that had flattered us.

And the thing worth carrying out of our own numbers: 13 of 13 of our registered labs wrote the bar down, and at least 9 registered items across 6 of them were never read back. Writing it down is the cheap half. The discipline is not self-executing — not theirs, and not ours.
