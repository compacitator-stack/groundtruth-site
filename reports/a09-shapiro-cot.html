VERDICT — The version anyone can build from his free videos showed no positioning edge

We wrote two plans on 2026-06-19 and tested each half of his rule on its own. The crowding signal, in five futures markets taken one at a time, over the four weeks after a crowded week: prices rose +0.23% on average after the big speculators were crowded short and +0.62% after they were crowded long, a gap of -0.39 points (95% interval -0.83 to +0.07). With the stretch of crude oil where our data switched contracts taken out, both came to +0.45% and the gap rounds to zero (-0.00, interval -0.41 to +0.42). The plan’s first bar needed that gap above zero with its interval clear of zero. It missed both ways, and the plan’s word for that outcome is fail. The reversal-day trigger, tested without the crowding check, on jobs-report days: 52 signals in 21 years, an average of +0.868 per dollar of risk over the next month, with a 95% interval from -0.691 to +2.431, which includes zero. In plain percent, in a check we ran afterwards, the trigger without the crowding check sat at the 36.2nd percentile of random days. Its own plan said in advance that a fail there would not count against his method. We first recorded this wrongly. In June our code added the two sides where the plan said subtract, and for three months our notes said the crowding signal had a small tilt his way. We found the error on 2026-09-24 and the correction was ruled the same day (§1). The ceiling: this is a verdict on the version we could build, five markets one at a time. It is not a verdict on Jason Shapiro, and not on the design he describes trading, the most crowded few of the thirty-seven markets he watches.

What we granted, and what we actually tested

Granted, not measured: Jason Shapiro is a long-tenured, respected practitioner: Jack Schwager profiled him as the contrarian in Unknown Market Wizards (2020). By his own account he has traded futures for more than thirty years and has used this report as his main data set for more than twenty (§6). He publishes his method free on his channel, Crowded Market Report. His own doctrine is more cautious than the internet’s version of it: he says “nobody sees the future”, that the report “sets you up in good risk reward trades that's it”, and he encourages people not to copy his trades. His record claim, no down year in twenty years, is his claim; we cannot check it, and nothing here needs it to be false.

Tested: The crowding half. Each week of the positioning data, January 2000 to June 2026, the large speculators’ net position in five futures markets (the S&P 500 E-mini, gold, WTI crude oil, the 10-year Treasury note and the euro), ranked against up to 156 weeks of that market’s own positions, ending with that week (fewer in each market’s first three years: the ranking starts once 20 weeks exist). The bottom 10% counted as crowded short and the top 10% as crowded long. After a crowded-short week the test bought at the first closing price on or after the positioning date plus three days, which our engine took to be the Friday the report came out: a Friday close for 1,954 of the 2,072 crowded weeks, and the next close after a Friday with no price for 72; for 15 crowded weeks whose positioning date was a Monday, the Thursday close, the day before that week’s Friday; for 20 crowded weeks reported before a market’s daily prices begin, the first price there is; and for 11 others a Monday, Tuesday or Wednesday close, up to 11 days after that date (§3). After a crowded-long week it bet on a fall. The result is the price change over the next four weeks, the plan’s primary hold, for each market on its own and for the five pooled. The trigger half, without the crowding check. On the first Friday of each month (our rule for the jobs report), a day whose low went below the previous day’s closing price and which then closed above both that price and its own opening price. Entry at that day’s closing price; the result over the next 21 trading days, counted in units of the distance from the entry down to that day’s low. No stop was executed; that low is the ruler. Both halves together. A crowded-short week in the S&P plus a reversal day, which the plan registered as its own check.

What this report does NOT claim: anything about Jason Shapiro’s own trading, record or character; the design he describes trading, the most crowded few of the thirty-seven markets he watches at any time; news other than the monthly jobs report, the only release our trigger used; his exits and his stops; his use of the report to judge the odds and the risk-reward, including whether a crowded reading helps on the risk side, on which we measured nothing; anything he sells; any market other than these five; and anything about returns after dividends or inflation: every figure is a price change in nominal terms, before costs except where a bar names its cost allowance.

The falsifiable question

Two, one per half, each with its bars written first. The crowd: over the next four weeks, do prices rise more after the big speculators are crowded short than after they are crowded long, with the gap’s interval clear of zero and the result beating random weeks? The trigger, without the crowding check: over the next 21 trading days, does a jobs-report reversal day drift up with its interval clear of zero, and beat random days?

  • A yes looks like: each plan’s PASS: all three bars met. The result clear of zero, above the 90th percentile of the random controls, and still positive after costs and the plan’s own exit; for the crowd, also at least half the markets pointing the same way.
  • A no looks like: each plan’s FAIL: the first bar missed.
  • Ambiguous residue: each plan’s WEAK: the first bar met and the second missed, which reads as what the market does anyway, not the signal. For the trigger, its plan also said in advance that a fail could not count against his method: it would only say where any edge has to live, in the crowding check or in his judgment.

Our stated prior: one line, in the trigger plan

The trigger plan’s section on a possible second phase says in passing that a fail was “likely for the standalone trigger” (line 143), meaning the trigger tested without the crowding check; the plan was last written before its run. The crowd plan states no expected outcome. The trigger plan also says in advance how each outcome is to be read; the crowd plan says a fail closes the test of the version anyone can build from his free material.

§3 · Was it the path? The stress tests

Several things could make this result an artifact of how we ran it. We checked what we could and name what we could not. Every interval on this page for the crowd treats each crowded week as independent, but crowded weeks come in streaks whose forward windows overlap, so those intervals are too narrow; that makes passing easier, not harder. The trigger, tested without the crowding check, fires at most once a month, and the lab did not measure whether its intervals are too narrow.

Edge or weather: the trigger’s one year, without the crowding check

Tested without the crowding check, the trigger’s 52 signals added up to +45.1 units of risk, and +31.6 of that came from 2016 alone, 70%. Without its best year, the trigger without the crowding check averages +0.287 per dollar of risk (47 signals) instead of +0.868. The plan asked for a second concentration check, the average without the single best trade: the engine prints it, the June write-up left it out, and the lab’s 2026-09-24 re-run of the unchanged engine prints +0.542 per dollar of risk for the trigger without the crowding check, reported on this page for the first time. Split in two, the trigger without the crowding check averaged -0.138 in 2005–2015 (27 signals) and +1.955 in 2016–2026 (25). Its two registered variants and its short mirror, all tested without the crowding check, are in the trigger table in §7; each one’s interval includes zero.

Two flaws in the positioning data

The 10-year note’s positioning series in our saved copy of the report ends on 2022-02-01, so that market covers about 22 years, not 26. Crude oil’s series is the New York WTI contract until 2022-02-01 and then, from 2022-02-08, a WTI contract on a different exchange: our code matched markets by name, the regulator renamed its markets that week, and the second contract was joined onto the first. Ranked against the old contract’s much larger positions, the new one often reads as crowded short: 64 of crude’s 137 crowded-short weeks fall after the switch, 48 of them in the three years when the ranking mixed the two contracts. We report both flaws and repair neither; the crude one we can step around, so every crowd result is shown as run and with crude’s weeks after the switch taken out.

Where the prices begin, and the ranking’s first three years

The early-price flaw

The report counts positions as of Tuesday (in our saved copy, 104 of its 6,667 weekly rows carry a Monday, Wednesday or Friday date instead) and comes out on Friday at 3:30 pm New York time. The crowd test entered at the daily closing price dated that Friday as our engine reckons it, the positioning date plus three days, or at the next price after it (for 15 crowded weeks whose positioning date was a Monday, at the Thursday close, the day before that Friday; before a market’s daily prices begin, at the first one there is; see above). For each market we asked at what time of day that closing price is fixed, by matching it against the same day’s five-minute prices over about the last 59 days of the feed. The method recovers the known 4:00 pm close of the S&P cash index. In three of the five markets (gold, crude oil and the 10-year note) the price was fixed before the report was public, and most likely in the euro too, where the method is weakest; only the S&P leg traded after it (one of its 417 crowded weeks is among the 15 entered at a Thursday close).

§5 · The case against our own test

The strongest case against our test, in four points. These are our words, not his, and every one of them is fair. We sort them by what has a run behind it.

  1. Breadth. By his own account he looks at thirty-seven markets and trades only the few showing the most crowded positioning, and at any one time that is a small handful. We ran five markets one at a time and took every crowded week. Our June result file itself calls our design the weaker one. Never run. It is a new test, with its own plan written first, and clean prices for thirty-seven futures markets are a cost we have not worked out.
  2. One kind of news. In the example he likes to give, the bad news was an inflation number; our trigger used only the jobs report, and a date rule that picked the wrong day 34 times in 242. Partly run: only the jobs report was ever run, and no other kind of news was tested. The date rule was checked, and the trigger, re-run without the crowding check on the real jobs-report dates, still missed its first bar. Both halves together fired three times in 21 years, too few to read.
  3. What the report is for. By his own account the report tells you where the odds sit and sets up the risk-reward, and the news is how he picks the turn (§6). Every bar we ran asked about the average price move after a crowded week; none asked about the risk side, such as how deep the losses ran or whether a stop survived. Never run. We have measured nothing on the risk side and cannot tell you he is right or wrong about it.
  4. We traded early. In three of the five markets our entry price was fixed before the report was public, and most likely in a fourth, the euro (§3). Confirmed by a check for three, most likely for the euro; effect not measured. If fading the crowd worked, the head start could only have helped it, and the crowding signal still failed.

Why trust the fix, when we got the first version wrong? Because the fix is checkable without trusting us: the shuffled-timing receipt in §4 shows what the June number measured, a blind reviewer reached the same four points unprompted, the June engine and its result file sit unchanged beside the corrected one, and the ledger below is the file the figures come from.

§8 · What would change our mind

  1. His design. Each week, find the most crowded of about thirty-seven markets and trade only those, across the full calendar of news releases, with a fresh plan written before the run. If it comes back positive, this verdict narrows to the one-market-at-a-time version.
  2. The S&P on data it has never seen. It leaned his way (+1.02 points, at the 100th and 98th percentiles of its two nulls) and is the one market whose daily closing price is fixed after the 3:30 pm release (one of its 417 crowded weeks was entered at a Thursday close; §3). But we singled it out after seeing all five, with intervals over 93 streaks of crowded weeks that are too narrow; only a new test on unseen data, planned first, can make it evidence.
  3. Our data, repaired. Re-fetch the renamed positioning series, freeze the prices, enter only at prices fixed after 3:30 pm, and re-run the corrected test unchanged.
  4. Both halves together, with enough signals to read. The full calendar of releases across many markets, so the combined setup fires often enough; that calendar costs money.

§9 · What survives

The report itself survives. It is free, it is real, and every week it shows you where the big speculators stand. His advice on stops was never on trial here, and nothing we found argues with it.

What did not survive, on the version we could build, is the idea that a crowded reading on its own tells you which way to trade next. The check to carry out of here is the one that measured our own number: when someone shows you a chart of trades taken after crowded readings, ask what the same trades would have made on random weeks. When a number beats zero but cannot beat random weeks, believe the random weeks.
