Is JEPI Worth It? We Measured It Against the Thing It’s Made Of

Episode D02 · report format v2 · lab run 2011-10-31 → 2026-06-30, each fund over its own life, receipt re-derived into this repo 2026-08-15, corrected and re-run 2026-09-05
VERDICT — NOT A KILL — A DISPERSION. MEASURED AS A SIMPLE YEARLY RATE, EVERY WRAPPER COST SOMETHING, AND WHAT IT COST RUNS FROM 2.1 POINTS A YEAR TO A FUND WHERE A DOLLAR BECAME $1.28 AGAINST $2.07 IN ITS OWN STOCK

Measured over each fund’s own full life against the thing it owns, and measured as a simple yearly rate — the published column, an annualized arithmetic mean — 0 of 21 measured pairs kept pace with their underlying. The ruler has to be said out loud, because it decides one row: counted the way money compounds (geometric), 1 of 21 came out ahead — MSTY, by 3.9 points a year over a 2.3-year life with no bear market inside it — and on dollars its $1.17 beat its stock’s $1.07. §5 owns that row. But the sign is not the finding — the size is, and it is wildly dispersed. At the top of the ladder the cheapest call-writers cost between 2.2 and 2.8 points a year (TSPY, XDTE, QDTE), and a Goldman Sachs fund sitting just below that band, GPIX, gave up 3.3 points a year and grew net asset value 13.8% a year while doing it. At the bottom, CONY: the published figure is −48.4% a year — and because that column is an annualized arithmetic mean rather than a compounding rate, the honest way to state that fund is what a dollar actually became: $1.28, against $2.07 for the stock it sells calls on. In between sits JEPI, the $44.3B fund that defines the category, which paid a life yield of 8.8% a year and trailed SPY by 7.9 points a year across its whole 6.1-year life (7.1 points counted geometrically): the yield you were paid and the return you gave up are within a point of each other. Those are two independently measured quantities that came out near each other. We are not saying the yield causes the gap — this receipt does not license that, and §2 says so at length. In the form no annualization convention can touch: a dollar in JEPI became $1.83 and a dollar in SPY became $2.67, same 6.1 years, same money in.

What we granted, and what we actually tested

Granted, not measured: that the cash is real and it arrives. These are large, regulated products from JPMorgan, Goldman Sachs, Global X, NEOS, Roundhill and Schwab, doing on the page exactly what their prospectuses say they do. We also grant, because our own table says so, that some of them are genuinely good: the cheapest call-writers on this page cost between 2.2 and 2.8 points a year, and the two Goldman funds just below that band sell calls on only part of what they hold. And we grant that sequence-of-returns protection is a real benefit — spending a distribution instead of selling into a falling market is worth something to somebody who is spending. None of that is what we measured.

Tested: one question, applied identically to every fund: did the fund’s total return over its own life keep pace with the thing it is made of? 24 funds; 21 of them have a named underlying on the source table and are measured against it over the identical window, month for month. Survivorship-free month-end price and distribution records through 2026-06-30, month-end net asset value plus distributions per share, split-consistent, the same method applied to all 24 so the columns are cross-comparable. No trading, no friction: this is an ownership comparison, not a strategy backtest.

What this report does NOT claim: that these funds are a category to be avoided, that any fund is dishonest, or that we know where any distribution was actually funded from. We have never read a fund’s ledger. “Your own money coming back” is a decomposition inference from net asset value drift against total return — a strong one, and corroborated at contract level (§6) — but it is an inference and it is labelled as one everywhere on this page. We also do not claim that the young funds’ records will hold, that this measures leverage (it does not; these are unlevered holdings), that it measures tax, or that it tells you what to own. And “covered-call funds are all bad” is not a claim this report makes: it is false against our own table, and the row that falsifies it is printed below.

§1 · What stands in place of a pre-registration

To be plain: there is no pre-registration for D02, and there is no lock hash — and that sentence sits where a lock hash would sit, rather than a hash we do not have. This episode is an explainer of a measurement already taken, not an experiment: the numbers existed before the episode did, so there was no open question to wager on and nothing to pre-commit. Our C02 and A13 reports can cite a pre-run lock hash; this one cannot, and manufacturing one would be the exact move this channel was built to catch.

What stands in its place is a provenance disclosure and a re-derivation, and the disclosure comes first because it is unusual for this channel: the receipt behind this page was computed outside this repo — a separate engine, publishing to a separate reference page. This pass re-derived it here, from the same survivorship-free store, cell by cell, on 2026-08-15, corrected and re-run 2026-09-05. The seven checks below are that re-derivation, each printed with the question it asked and the denominator it closed. Three of them did not come back clean, and all three are recorded rather than repaired quietly.

The falsifiable question

Over each fund’s own life, did the fund’s total return keep pace with the thing it is made of — and if not, by how much?

  • A yes looks like: a fund whose total return matches or beats its own underlying over its whole life. Across 21 measured pairs, measured as a simple yearly rate, that happened 0 of 21 times — the smallest gap on the table is SCHD at −2.1% a year, a dividend fund rather than a call-writer; the smallest call-writer gap is TSPY at −2.2%. Counted geometrically it happened 1 of 21 times (MSTY, §5).
  • A no looks like: a gap that is large and roughly uniform across the category, which is the only shape of result that would license a flat verdict on covered-call funds as a class.
  • Ambiguous residue: a gap that is always there but wildly dispersed — which is exactly where the measurement landed, and it is why the verdict below is a dispersion rather than a kill. The published range across the 21 pairs runs −2.1% to −48.4% a year — that bottom figure on the published basis, which is an annualized arithmetic mean rather than a compounding rate (§5), and it is read below as what a dollar became.

What we did not have going in — and the three scars from re-deriving the receipt inside this repo

Scar one: six of the 24 rows had no engine entry. The engine is hard-bound to its fund specification table, and the companion file the reference page’s own provenance block names is not on disk anywhere. The affected rows are CONY, NVDY, QYLD, RYLD, TSLY, XYLD — which includes QYLD, the longest and most load-bearing record on this page. We re-declared the 6 specifications from the source table’s own left-hand column and re-derived them from the same store: 24 of 24 master cells and 6 of 6 cap-cost cells reproduce. ⚠ That is a reconstruction of the spec, not a recovery of the file. It proves the figures are re-derivable; it does not prove the vanished file used this exact pairing. Agreement to print precision on 30 of 30 cells is the evidence that it did, and it is the only evidence there is.

Scar two: the columns labelled “CAGR” are not geometric CAGRs. Both — NAV CAGR and Total CAGR — are annualized arithmetic means, and those coincide with a geometric rate only at zero volatility. The total-return pair is printed both ways below; the NAV rate only as published, and no geometric NAV rate is on this page (§7). We found this in our own instrument, it is one of the two most important caveats on this page, and it has its own section (§5) rather than a footnote.

Scar three: the index leg was measured over one month too many, on every pair. Every one of the 21 fund-vs-index cells carried one extra month on the index side — the month before the fund’s first return, a month the fund did not exist for — and the reference page carried the same month, which is why the re-derivation reproduced it perfectly. Reproducing is not the same as being right. Fixed 2026-09-05: 21 of 21 pairs now carry the same count of monthly returns over the same month-ends on both legs. On the 17 index pairs the correction moved the published gap by up to 2.17 points a year; on the 4 single-stock pairs by up to 74.73 points; and on MSTY it flipped the sign of the geometric gap (§5). The corrected table is the one on this page, and the reference page was corrected the same day.

§3 · Was it the weather? The regime boundary

17 of the 24 funds on this page have never seen a bear market. That is not a caveat riding under the table — it is the shape of the evidence. Seven funds have any real drawdown regime inside their life, as our reference page’s own regime caveat names them (QYLD, XYLD, RYLD, JEPI, SCHD, DIVO, TSLY): the three old buy-writes, JEPI, the two dividend funds, and one single-stock fund whose own stock crashed on its watch. The rest lived entirely inside the run that followed. Their positive net-asset-value records are bull-flattered, and a two-year record is a statement about the weather.

What real regime evidence exists on this tape: JEPI’s max drawdown −18.9% through 2022, and QYLD over 12.5 years with a max drawdown of −39.3% and a net-asset-value path of −2.0% a year. The two other old-guard buy-writes tell the same story: XYLD net asset value −1.3% a year over 5.8 years and RYLD −5.2% a year with a −44.0% drawdown.

The converse is ours to say as well: a young fund with a good number has not been disproved by anything here. It has simply not been tested. Those are different, and the reopen bounty in §8 is written against exactly that gap.

§4 · The controls

Each fund’s control is its own underlying, over its own window, computed by the same method. That is what makes the columns comparable and it is the entire instrument: SPY re-derives at +18.9% a year over JEPI’s 73 monthly returns and QQQ at +21.4% a year over QYLD’s 150. Change the window and both sides change together — which is exactly the property the extra index month in §1 broke, on every pair, until 2026-09-05.

The two dividend portfolios on the table are the second control. SCHD, which writes no calls, grew net asset value +10.5% a year over 14.7 years, and DIVO, which sells calls on only part of its book, +7.5% a year over 9.5 years, both through full cycles. If durable growth with a payout was the thing you wanted, that is where our own table points — and neither one is a high-yield product.

And the negative control on the metric: the four funds this report leans on hardest have almost no variance drag — JEPI 0.56pp · SPYI 0.62pp · QYLD 0.64pp · GPIX 0.68pp — so §5’s finding does not touch the spine of this page. It was checked before it was relied on, not after. That check covered each fund’s own return, not the gaps; JEPI’s gap counted both ways is in §2.

§5 · The best objection — and it is ours, against our own instrument

“Your headline column is not the statistic you think it is.” It is the best objection to this page, nobody raised it against us, and it is true. Two of the reference table’s columns are labelled “CAGR” — NAV CAGR and Total CAGR — and the engine computes each as an annualized arithmetic mean of monthly changes. An arithmetic mean and a geometric rate coincide only at zero volatility, and for a violently volatile fund the published figure sits well above what a holder actually compounded.

We measured the gap on the total-return column — the one the cap cost rests on — for every fund rather than arguing about it. Median across the 24 funds: 1.04 points a year. And on the wildest one the ruler flips the sign. MSTY’s published gap against its own stock is −17.5% a year (annualized arithmetic mean) — a loss. A 144.9% life yield produced $1.17; the label told you nothing either way.

Two things this does not mean. It does not mean the annualized arithmetic mean is the wrong statistic in general: for a distribution rate — cash paid out, never compounded — it is arguably the honest one, which is why the life-yield column is left exactly as published. And it does not touch the spine of this report: the four funds it leans on hardest carry drags of JEPI 0.56pp · SPYI 0.62pp · QYLD 0.64pp · GPIX 0.68pp. We found the defect in our own instrument, we published the size of it, and we changed how 6 rows may be spoken about. We did not delete them.

§6 · Their own terms, and our evidence classes

The mechanism is not in this receipt. This page measures a gap. It does not prove that selling calls is what caused the gap, because nothing here reads a fund’s books. What we hold instead is a contract-level prior from our own options lab, published in an earlier episode, and it is a prior, not an answer: writing weekly calls on QQQ at the bid over six years collected $327 per share in premium and handed back $429 per share in intrinsic value on capped weeks, and at the five-delta limit the overlay finished +192.9% against +193.7% for simply holding, winning from 7 of 52 start dates. That is a self-managed overlay on one underlying. It contains no net asset value, no distribution, no return of capital, no expense ratio and no fund. Reasoning from it to a fund is reasoning, and it is labelled as reasoning wherever it appears.

The numbers here that are not ours. Four columns are carried from stockanalysis.com as-of 2026-07-01 — expense ratio, AUM, inception and distribution frequency — and none of them can be re-derived from a price-and-distribution store. The AUM column is the one that reaches the prose: it sums to at least $139.5B across 22 call-writing funds (JEPI $44.3B · JEPQ $39.0B · QQQI $12.5B), and it ships as a floor on the category rather than as the category: 24 funds is not the universe, and we did not count the universe.

The three outside sources the episode cites are witnesses, not specimens — and their figures are deliberately absent from this report. The episode credits an income educator who publishes his own book, a retail holder who publishes his dashboard, and an independent analyst who reconciled one fund’s published trade files for one month. Every figure of theirs is theirs, is credited to them on screen and in the description, and is not a measurement of ours — so none of it is carried here, where the whole point is that every figure on the page came out of the same instrument. The subject of this test is a fund structure. It is not a person, and no person appears in this report’s title, verdict or headline.

And the honest scars on the sample. Six of the 24 rows had to be re-declared from the source table’s own left-hand column before they could be re-derived at all (§1). They reproduce to print precision on 30 of 30 cells, which is the evidence, and it is a reconstruction of the spec, not a recovery of the file. And every fund-vs-index cell on our reference page carried one extra index month until 2026-09-05; this page carries the corrected cells, and says so.

§7 · The complete ledger — all 24 rows

§8 · What would change our mind

  1. A real bear market on the young funds. 17 of these 24 funds have never seen one. If the newer anchors come through a genuine full drawdown cycle with flat-or-better net asset value and a wrapper cost inside about two points a year, then our own words about them — that they are structurally the same machine as the old buy-writes with a shorter, kinder sample — are wrong. That puts them on the top rung of our ladder, we re-run the table, and we say so on camera.
  2. The fund-ledger reconciliation. This is the cheaper one and it would upgrade our evidence class rather than reverse it. Two of these fund families publish their own trade files; matching options profit and loss against the distributions paid, fund by fund, would move this page’s central claim from a decomposition inference to the funds’ own books. It is a document fetch plus arithmetic. We did not run it, it does not block this report, and we would rather have it than not.
  3. A call-writing fund that beat the thing it is made of over its own full life, on every ruler. Measured as a simple yearly rate, 0 of the 21 pairs here did; counted the way money compounds, 1 did — MSTY, by 3.9 points a year, over a 2.3-year life with no bear market inside it (§5). Send us one that did it on every ruler, with a named underlying and a full life through a real drawdown, not a hand-picked window — and we run it here first.

§9 · What survives

A yield means nothing until it is standing next to the thing the fund owns. That comparison is the entire instrument on this page, it is subtraction rather than opinion, and it is the one number no fund’s own marketing page is responsible for printing.

The wrapper always costs something; the only question is how much. Measured as a simple yearly rate, 0 of 21 kept pace. The cost ran from 2.1 points a year to a fund where a dollar became $1.28 against $2.07 in its own stock, and the headline yield did not tell you which one you had bought. The cheapest call-writers cost between 2.2 and 2.8 points a year; the two Goldman funds just below them gave up 3.3 and 4.8, and both sell calls on only part of the book — the modest version of this product, on our own table.

Ask what a dollar became, not what the rate says. A multiple carries no annualization convention, so it cannot be inflated by one — and we needed that rule because of a defect in our own column, not in anybody else’s. It also decided one row the rate got wrong (§5).

A fund with no bear market inside its life has a record about the weather. 17 of these 24 have never been in one. 11 of the 24 grew net asset value while paying you; 8 of those 11 did it entirely inside a rising market.

What the wrapper actually is, on this evidence: a paid drawdown dampener and a bounded decumulation tool. If you are spending the distributions rather than reinvesting them, that is worth something real. It is not a wealth engine, and it was never built to be one.
